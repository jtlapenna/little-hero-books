# LHL outreach anti-patterns

Things to **never do** in outreach drafts. Each one came up explicitly in Jeff's feedback on the tone co-creation drafts (or in the brand voice rules in CLAUDE.md). Hard nos.

---

## Format / mechanical anti-patterns

### Em dashes (—)
Never. Anywhere. In subject lines, body, signoffs, anywhere.

> ❌ "Your child becomes the hero — and looks like them too."
> ✅ "Your child becomes the hero, and looks like them too."
> ✅ "Your child becomes the hero. They look like them too."

Substitutes: periods, commas, colons, parentheses.

### Walls of text
DMs and emails without paragraph breaks read as form letters. Always break by idea.

> ❌ One 250-word block.
> ✅ Three to five short paragraphs. Two-line max for DMs.

### AI vocabulary words
The humanizer skill has the full list. Most common offenders for outreach: *delve, leverage, tapestry, pivotal, intricate, underscore, fostering, garner, in the heart of, vibrant*. Strip these.

### Curly quotes
Straight quotes only ("..." not "..."). Curly quotes are an AI tell that gets pasted in from any AI-assisted draft tool.

### Subject-line clichés
> ❌ "Quick question for you" (vague, looks like spam)
> ❌ "Following up" (without prior contact)
> ❌ Title Case Of Every Word
> ✅ Sentence case, specific to the ask: "Gifted book pitch for [Channel Name]"

## Voice / framing anti-patterns

### Specific-reference opener as the default
*"I read your essay on X and it's been rattling around my head…"* / *"Watched your reel three times…"* / *"Your post on Y stuck with me…"*

Jeff's read: feels disingenuous, screams "cold outreach marketing." Default to the founder-direct opener. Specific-reference goes in the A/B variant only, used sparingly.

### Mechanical product description ("build the character")
Don't describe the *process* of personalization. Describe the *outcome*.

> ❌ "Parents build the actual character — hair style, skin tone, favorite color, animal sidekick."
> ❌ "You customize all the visual attributes."
> ✅ "Your child becomes the hero, illustrated to look like them. Our first title, *Finding Our Inner Voice*, is about learning to hear and listen to your inner voice."
> ✅ "Your child is literally the protagonist of the story."

### Repetitive framing within the same email
If you've said "your child is the hero on every page" in paragraph 1, don't say it again in paragraph 2. Vary the wording.

### "I do X, you do Y" corny content-creator framing
> ❌ "I'd cover the book, you cover the magic."
> ❌ "I bring the product, you bring the audience."
> ❌ "We build, you share."

Just describe what's actually proposed.

> ✅ "I'd send a personalized copy. You'd film the read-aloud however feels natural to your style."

### Founder-letter style (too long, beats around the bush)
A 250-word email that takes 3 paragraphs to get to the ask is too long. Default to 100–180 words for emails. The personal-touch backstory is rarely worth the word count.

### Pretending this isn't business outreach
Trying to make a cold pitch feel like a personal note from a friend reads as artifice. Be transparent.

> ✅ "We're running partner campaigns and looking for a few more creators."
> ❌ Hiding the business motivation behind a long warm-up.

## Offer / business anti-patterns

### Naming offer specifics in initial outreach
Never. **Not the dollar amounts. Not the mechanism. Not even the high-level structure.** The first message gestures at a partnership conversation existing; specifics come *after* the creator expresses interest *and* audience quality is internally confirmed.

> ❌ "We typically pay $150–500 flat fee."
> ❌ "We offer a custom discount code rev share or a small flat fee."
> ❌ "Compensation is gifted plus a small flat fee."
> ✅ "If you like the book, I'll share more about our campaign offers."
> ✅ "If you enjoy the book, I'll share details about our partner program."

**Why:** sharing offer specifics upfront commits to mechanics before audience fit is known, and it signals a transactional pitch rather than a relationship-building one.

### Making competitor comparisons aggressive
Naming Wonderbly is uncertain (Jeff is open but flagged unsure). If invoking the comparison, do it as differentiation not as a swipe.

> 🤷 A/B variant: "Wonderbly lets parents pick from pre-made characters. We let them create their own."
> ❌ "Wonderbly is overpriced and you can't even see the illustrations."
> ❌ "Unlike [competitor]'s low-quality print…"

### Fabricated social proof
> ❌ "Our partners typically see 3× engagement."
> ❌ "Past creators have driven 200+ orders in the first week."
> ❌ "We've worked with influencers like [name-drop]."

If a metric, partner, or testimonial isn't real and verified, don't include it. Use possibility framing if needed: *"For an audience like yours, this typically means…"*

### Promising shipping timelines
Always defer to Amazon checkout estimate. Don't promise "arrives in 4 days" — say "typical 3–5 business days production; final estimate at checkout."

## Persona / signature anti-patterns

### "Echo" or any AI-persona name
Outgoing voice is **Jeff** or **Jeff at Little Hero Labs**. Never an assistant, never a "we" that hides who's writing.

### Corporate-speak signoff
> ❌ "Looking forward to potentially partnering with you."
> ❌ "Reach out anytime if there's an alignment."
> ❌ "Excited about the possibility of working together."
> ✅ "Let me know if you're open to it."
> ✅ "Happy to send a preview if that helps."

### Apologetic openers
> ❌ "Sorry to drop into your DMs out of nowhere…"
> ❌ "I know you're busy, but…"
> ❌ "Hopefully this isn't bothering you…"

These signal weakness and waste the first line. If the message is bothering them, an apology won't fix it. If it isn't, the apology creates one.

## When in doubt

If a draft feels off but the writer can't say why, run this checklist:

1. Em dashes → strip them
2. AI vocabulary words → strip them
3. Specific-reference opener → check it's actually specific, not generic praise; consider switching to founder-direct
4. "Build the character" or process-y language → reframe to outcome
5. Cute "I X, you Y" framings → cut and describe directly
6. Length → can it be 30% shorter and still complete?
7. Paragraph breaks → are they every 1–3 sentences?
8. Sign-off → is it Jeff, not an assistant or "we"?
9. Wonderbly comparison → is this an A/B variant or did it sneak in?
10. Sounds like a real person who has a product to pitch → or sounds like a marketing campaign?

If any of these fail, fix before presenting to Jeff.
